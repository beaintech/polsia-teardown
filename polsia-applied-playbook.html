Polsia, applied: what it means for us

Part 1 asks whether this tool is worth $20 to our business, and exactly how to use it if so. Part 2 asks what we would change if we built the competing version ourselves.

Companion to the earlier teardown. Diagram-first: every section opens with a figure, then explains it. All diagrams are inline vector, so this file works offline and prints cleanly.

The one-line answer to "should we pay the $20"

Not as a place to run a business. Yes as disposable ammunition for testing twenty ideas — but only if it never touches our name, our customers, or our code.

Part 1 · Our business

Is it worth $20 to us, and how would we use it

Answer the three questions below honestly and the decision makes itself.

Why "no" is the honest answer for our main business

We already have the agent layer. The orchestration is a commodity — OpenClaw has 375k GitHub stars and a 933-contributor release as of this week. That is the part Polsia built, and it is the part we would be renting.

We would be paying 20% of revenue for something we can host ourselves. For a team that writes code, this is the worst-value tier in the entire market. The take rate is priced for people who cannot build.

Its weakness is exactly our strength. Its documented failure is building before validating. Judgement and validation are the parts we are actually good at. Renting a tool that skips the step we are best at is backwards.

So what is the $20 actually for?

Not for running a business. For buying twenty cheap shots at finding out whether an idea has any signal — and then rebuilding the one that does, properly, on our own stack.

Worth doing with it

  • Idea shotgunning. Twenty ideas at $20 each, run for thirty days, keep the one that gets a response. Nothing else on the market makes this cheap.
  • Reverse engineering. $20 for a working reference implementation of agent orchestration, cross-company memory and multi-line monetisation. Cheap research.
  • Testing demand in a market we do not know. If we want to know whether anyone wants a niche product, letting a throwaway brand test it costs us nothing and risks nothing.

Never do with it

  • Point it at our existing brand. Agents send cold email and post publicly in the name they are given. Wrong prices and invented claims are documented.
  • Put anything we want to keep on it. Code and domains sit on Polsia's infrastructure. Users report difficulty getting them back.
  • Treat a "complete" task as done. One documented log: 47 tasks, 21.3% actually worked. Verify everything ourselves before believing any of it.
Part 2 · If we build it

What we would change, and where the open ground is

The one that matters most: the validation gate

Polsia's central design flaw is not that it builds badly. It is that it builds before anyone has asked for anything. If we build one thing differently, it is this.

One correction to our own plan

I had assumed we should replace the take rate with pure outcome pricing. The data says otherwise. Across 30+ vendors, Bain found 65% use hybrid pricing and exactly zero are fully outcome-based — pure outcome pricing leaves too much revenue on the table when the agent works and the customer still will not pay.

So the right model is flat subscription + credits + an outcome component that only starts above a revenue threshold and is capped. It keeps the alignment story Polsia claims, without the permanent 20% that its customers hate.

What I would build first, and what I would skip

Ship in v1

  • Validation gate with the two checks. This is the product.
  • Bring-your-own GitHub and cloud. Cheap for us to build, and it is the thing competitors in this category do not offer.
  • Level 2 autonomy as default, with the approval queue.
  • A verifier that runs a real check — HTTP 200, deploy succeeded, email delivered — and refuses to mark a task complete otherwise.

Deliberately skip

  • The marketplace and funds roadmap. Polsia's deck says "not a tool, an economy." That is a Series B story, not a v1.
  • Cross-company memory at scale. It is Polsia's only real moat, but it needs thousands of companies before it means anything.
  • Ad management. Meta and Google ad automation is a compliance and support minefield. Let someone else own that surface.
  • Being general purpose. Pick one vertical — independent developers and consultants is the one we actually understand.

The honest summary

Polsia proved the category and the demand are real. It also proved that demand and satisfaction are two different things — 94% abandonment and a 1.8-star rating from the same product that raised $30M.

What it has not built is the thing that would fix that: asking whether anyone wants the thing before building it. That is one box in a diagram, nobody has built it, and it is the difference between selling activity and selling outcomes.
